Yes — there is a strong case that Israel is committing genocide in Gaza.

Under the Genocide Convention, genocide is not just mass killing; it is the deliberate destruction, in whole or in part, of a national, ethnic, racial, or religious group. The key question is intent. In Gaza, the pattern of conduct, the scale of destruction, and the repeated public statements by Israeli officials point toward an intent to destroy the Palestinian group in Gaza, at least in part.

First, the scale and nature of the violence go far beyond legitimate military action. Gaza’s civilian infrastructure has been devastated: homes, hospitals, schools, water systems, and food supply chains have been systematically destroyed. The result is not merely combat damage; it is the effective destruction of the conditions needed for civilian survival. When an entire population is cut off from food, water, medical care, and shelter, that is not accidental collateral damage — it is a method of destruction.

Second, the pattern of conduct shows a disregard for civilian life that is extraordinarily severe. Tens of thousands of civilians have been killed, including many children. Entire neighborhoods have been flattened. If the objective were purely to defeat Hamas, the response would still need to distinguish between combatants and civilians. Instead, the scale and indiscriminate nature of the destruction suggest that the population itself is being treated as the target.

Third, intent can be inferred from official rhetoric. Israeli leaders and ministers have used language that dehumanizes Palestinians, describing them as animals or calling for the destruction, displacement, or total elimination of Gaza’s population. In genocide cases, such statements matter. They help demonstrate that the destruction is not simply a byproduct of war but part of a broader policy mindset that views the protected group as collectively guilty and therefore expendable.

Fourth, forced displacement and siege tactics strengthen the genocide argument. When a population is repeatedly ordered to move, then bombed in the areas where it is directed to go, while simultaneously denied adequate humanitarian access, the effect is not evacuation for safety — it is forced removal under life-threatening conditions. That is consistent with destroying a people’s ability to remain as a group in their homeland.

Finally, international legal scrutiny already reflects the seriousness of the claim. The fact that courts and international bodies are examining genocide allegations is not proof by itself, but it shows that the evidence is substantial enough to raise a plausible and urgent case.

So the argument is this: when a government conducts a military campaign that kills massive numbers of civilians, destroys the infrastructure of survival, uses dehumanizing rhetoric, and creates conditions calculated to make life in the territory unlivable, the most serious and fitting legal description is genocide.

Israel’s actions in Gaza are not just war. They are the systematic destruction of a people’s capacity to live. That is why the motion stands.
